Turning coal-ash pond waste into ceramic water filters

Coal ash is usually stored in ponds that can leak into groundwater. AshPure tests that ash, dries it, mixes it with clay, and fires it into a porous ceramic. Water passes through the pores. Particles stay behind.

Two problems, one material

Coal power plants leave behind ash. Much of it is stored as a wet slurry in ponds. If a pond leaks, metals and other pollutants can reach the groundwater underneath.

At the same time, many communities need affordable ways to treat polluted water. Bricks and cement already reuse some ash. AshPure points the same waste at the second problem.

What it could replace

  • Conventional ceramic filter material.
  • Some of the clay, silica or alumina used to make filters.
  • Some disposable plastic filter cartridges, where a ceramic filter is suitable.

It would not replace every filter. Each design has to match the pollutant it removes.

From pond to filter

The ash has to come out of the pond first. Then it becomes a filter.

  1. Pump the ash slurry out of the pond.
  2. Let it settle in a thickening tank. Heavy ash sinks and clearer water rises.
  3. Send the separated water to treatment. It is never released straight into a river or the ground.
  4. Squeeze the thick ash in a belt filter press or filter press. The cloth lets water through and holds the ash as a solid cake.
  5. Dry the cake and test it for heavy metals, radioactivity and chemical makeup.
  6. Grind the ash and mix it with clay or sand, a pore-former such as sawdust or starch, a temporary binder, and water. Press it into disks, tubes, plates or honeycombs.
  7. Dry the shapes slowly so they don't crack, then fire them in a kiln at roughly 800–1,150 °C.
  8. Add an optional coating (activated carbon or zeolite) for specific pollutants. Then test pore size, strength, flow, pollutant removal and leaching.

What the kiln does

The sawdust or starch in the mix burns away. The ash and clay grains fuse together. The empty spaces left behind become pores, and they connect, so water can find a way through.

Particle removal is a smooth curve that rises as particles get larger compared with the pores. Flow follows a Kozeny–Carman-style relationship: it rises with porosity and pore size and falls with thickness, and it slows as the filter catches more. A coating removes dissolved lead at a starting rate that fades as it fills. The leaching check is qualitative. This is a simplified teaching model, not lab data. Every number would need to be measured on real, tested samples.

What AshPure can't do yet

  • It mainly removes particles and some larger contaminants. Dissolved salts, microbes and chemicals can pass through unless extra treatment layers are added.
  • Water that has passed through it is not automatically safe to drink.
  • Each filter has to be designed for the pollutant it targets.
  • Real coal ash can contain heavy metals. It has to be tested, and the fired filter has to be proven not to leach them.
  • Kiln firing uses energy, and the cost per filter has not been worked out.
  • Regulators would need to approve any ash-based material used in water treatment.

A safe classroom model

  • Use clay, fine sand, charcoal, starch or sawdust, and water. Never real ash.
  • Build two small filter columns: sand and gravel as the control, and clay, sand and charcoal as the AshPure model.
  • Pour colored water through both. Compare color, clarity, flow speed and how much passes through.
  • For the dewatering step, let a sand or clay slurry settle, pour off the top water, then filter the rest through a cloth or coffee filter.
  • Do not drink the filtered water.

AshPure is a research concept. The firing range and pore size shown here come from published studies on fly-ash ceramic membranes, which have used temperatures of roughly 800–1,150 °C and reported average pore sizes near 0.6 micrometres. Simulator results are illustrative and do not replace laboratory testing. Real coal ash must never be handled outside a professional, tested setting.
